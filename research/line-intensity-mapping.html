research · observational cosmology

line intensity mapping

Line intensity mapping (LIM) is an observational technique that measures the aggregate emission of spectral lines from large cosmological volumes, without resolving individual galaxies. Rather than detecting discrete sources one at a time, LIM surveys collect the total line emission from all galaxies — individually unresolved — within each spatial and spectral resolution element. The resulting three-dimensional maps trace the large-scale structure of the universe as a function of redshift, encoding statistical information about galaxy populations, star formation history, and cosmological evolution [Karkare et al. 2022].

why it matters

Traditional galaxy surveys detect individual sources above a flux threshold, which introduces selection biases and becomes prohibitively expensive at high redshift. LIM bypasses this limitation: by summing all emission within a volume — including from galaxies too faint to detect individually — it provides an unbiased tracer of the underlying matter distribution and star formation activity across cosmic time. This makes it a uniquely powerful tool for studying the epoch of peak cosmic star formation (redshift z ~ 1–3) and for constraining cosmological parameters at redshifts beyond the reach of conventional surveys.

A particularly important target line is singly ionized carbon ([CII] at 158 μm), one of the brightest emission lines in star-forming galaxies. Because the [CII] luminosity traces the instantaneous star formation rate, mapping its intensity as a function of redshift directly measures the cosmic star formation history in dust-obscured environments that are invisible to optical surveys [Vieira et al. 2020]. At millimeter wavelengths, the CO rotational ladder provides a complementary probe of the molecular gas that fuels star formation, accessible from ground-based observatories [Karkare et al. 2022].

Beyond galaxy evolution, LIM in the millimeter band can constrain cosmological parameters — including the expansion history of the universe and neutrino properties — at redshifts where galaxy surveys face significant challenges [Barry et al. 2023]. Cross-correlating LIM measurements with existing spectroscopic surveys and space missions like Euclid can further amplify scientific returns [Bracks et al. 2026].

the observational challenge

LIM experiments face two main technical challenges. First, the signal of interest is faint and must be extracted from a much brighter foreground of continuum emission and line interlopers from other redshifts. Second, far-infrared wavelengths — where [CII] and other key diagnostic lines are brightest — are inaccessible from the ground due to atmospheric opacity, requiring balloon-borne or space-based platforms. Millimeter wavelengths, where redshifted CO lines fall, are accessible from high-altitude ground-based sites, but require highly multiplexed spectrometers with thousands of detectors to survey adequate volumes. These technical demands make LIM a particularly active area of detector and instrumentation development.

current experiments

A growing suite of experiments is working to demonstrate LIM as a practical observational technique at both far-infrared and millimeter wavelengths. I am currently involved in two of them: TIM and SPT-SLIM.

TIM — Terahertz Intensity Mapper

Balloon-borne, far-infrared. Targeting [CII] at z = 0.5–1.7 from Antarctica.

SPT-SLIM

Ground-based, millimeter-wave. CO line intensity mapping pathfinder on the South Pole Telescope.

TIM: the Terahertz Intensity Mapper

TIM is a NASA-funded balloon-borne far-infrared imaging spectrometer designed to characterize the star formation history of dust-obscured galaxies around the peak of cosmic star formation. The atmosphere is largely opaque to terahertz photons, making stratospheric balloons the only platform capable of wide-area spectral mapping in this band from within the solar system. TIM will fly from Antarctica at an altitude of approximately 37 km [Vieira et al. 2020].

The instrument consists of a 2-meter low-emissivity carbon fiber telescope feeding two grating spectrometer modules that together cover 240–420 μm at a resolving power of R ~ 250, across a 1.3-degree field of view. The focal plane uses 7,200 aluminum kinetic inductance detectors (KIDs) read out through a novel RF system-on-chip architecture [Vieira et al. 2020]. TIM will map the redshifted [CII] 158 μm line over the redshift range z = 0.5–1.7, corresponding to lookback times of 5–10 billion years — the epoch over which the cosmic star formation rate rose to its peak and then declined. TIM is planned to fly from Antarctica in the 2027–2028 austral summer [Bracks et al. 2026].

By applying the LIM technique, TIM will measure the total [CII] intensity — including contributions from galaxies too faint to detect individually — providing a dust-immune census of star formation over half of cosmic history. TIM is also designed as a technology demonstrator for future far-infrared space missions, which would carry out similar science at much greater sensitivity [Vieira et al. 2020].

SPT-SLIM: South Pole Telescope Shirokoff Line Intensity Mapper

SPT-SLIM is a millimeter-wave LIM pathfinder experiment deployed on the 10-meter South Pole Telescope. Its goal is to demonstrate the performance of on-chip superconducting filter-bank spectrometers for LIM at millimeter wavelengths — technology that will underpin the next generation of large-scale LIM surveys [Karkare et al. 2022].

The SPT-SLIM focal plane consists of 18 dual-polarization pixels, each coupled to an R = 300 thin-film microstrip filter-bank spectrometer spanning the 2 mm atmospheric window at 120–180 GHz. Each spectral channel feeds a microstrip-coupled lumped-element KID, giving approximately 10,000 detectors in total. The cryostat holds the focal plane at 100 mK and is designed to mount on the South Pole Telescope without removing the primary SPT-3G receiver [Barry et al. 2021]. SPT-SLIM targets the CO rotational line emission at redshifts z = 0.5–2, probing the molecular gas content of galaxies over several billion years of cosmic history.

SPT-SLIM completed its first deployment during the 2024–2025 austral summer season. On-sky characterization, including atmospheric calibration and spectral bandpass measurements of the filter-bank spectrometers, was performed on site using a Fourier transform spectrometer [Dibert et al. 2025]. The techniques and detector technologies demonstrated by SPT-SLIM are intended to inform the design of future, larger-scale millimeter LIM experiments capable of constraining cosmology at redshifts beyond the reach of conventional galaxy surveys [Barry et al. 2023].
