the cosmic microwave background & the uhf small aperture telescope

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is the oldest light in the universe — thermal radiation released roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang, when the universe cooled enough for neutral hydrogen to form and the cosmos became transparent to photons. Its polarization encodes direct information about the inflationary epoch, making it one of the most powerful probes of early-universe physics accessible to us today.

B-mode polarization and inflation

CMB polarization decomposes into two components: curl-free E-modes and divergence-free B-modes. Scalar density perturbations generate only E-modes at linear order. Primordial gravitational waves — predicted by inflationary models — would imprint a distinct B-mode signal on degree angular scales (ℓ ~ 30–300). A detection would constitute direct evidence for inflation and measure the energy scale at which it occurred.

The primordial B-mode amplitude is parameterized by the tensor-to-scalar ratio r. Current upper limits place r < 0.036 at 95% confidence (BICEP/Keck 2021). The Simons Observatory targets σ(r) ≈ 0.003, an order-of-magnitude improvement probing a broad class of inflationary models [SO Collaboration 2019].

the Simons Observatory: nominal configuration

The Simons Observatory (SO) is located at 5,200 meters elevation on Cerro Toco in the Atacama Desert of northern Chile [SO Collaboration 2019]. In its nominal configuration, SO consists of one 6-meter large aperture telescope (LAT) and three 0.42-meter small aperture telescopes (SATs), fielding a total of approximately 68,000 transition-edge sensor (TES) bolometers across six frequency bands from 27 to 280 GHz.

the large aperture telescope

The LAT is a 6-meter crossed-Dragone telescope designed to map the CMB at arcminute resolution over approximately 40% of the sky. Its focal plane — the Large Aperture Telescope Receiver (LATR), the largest cryogenic CMB camera ever built — will ultimately house 13 optics tubes carrying more than 62,000 TES detectors across the six SO frequency bands [Xu et al. 2021, Bhandarkar et al. 2025]. The LATR is now fully populated with all 13 optics tubes, fielding over 62,000 detectors.

In February 2025, the LAT achieved first light with an observation of Mars, confirming end-to-end functionality [SO Collaboration 2025]. The LAT survey will cover about 60% of the sky at arcminute resolution, with five times the angular resolution and ten times the map depth of Planck.

The LAT science program is broad. At small angular scales, the LAT will measure gravitational lensing of the CMB, the thermal and kinematic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effects, and produce catalogs of more than 30,000 galaxy clusters and 100,000 extragalactic millimeter sources. These measurements will constrain the sum of neutrino masses, the properties of dark energy, and the growth of large-scale structure. At large scales, the LAT's high-resolution lensing maps will be used to delens the SAT B-mode measurements, improving constraints on primordial gravitational waves. The LAT will also constrain the number of light relativistic species in the early universe (Neff), probing physics beyond the Standard Model [SO Collaboration 2025].

the three small aperture telescopes

The three SATs share a common design: a 0.42-meter refractive aperture with a 35-degree field of view, a cryogenic rotating half-wave plate (CHWP) for polarization modulation, and TES detector arrays cooled to 100 mK. Each SAT observes in two frequency bands, covering degree-scale angular modes where the primordial B-mode signal peaks [Galitzki et al. 2021, Galitzki et al. 2024].

expansions to SO

Advanced Simons Observatory (ASO). The ASO program comprises two upgrades to the existing LAT infrastructure [SO Collaboration 2025]. The LATR has been fully populated with six additional optics tubes — four mid-frequency (90/150 GHz) and two UHF (225/280 GHz) — adding approximately 30,000 detectors and nearly doubling the LAT's mapping speed, along with an automated data reduction pipeline. Second, a large-scale photovoltaic array is being installed at the site, which will reduce reliance on diesel-generated power by approximately 70% and provide a more stable power system for the observatory.

SO:UK. Two additional mid-frequency SATs contributed by the United Kingdom, using kinetic inductance detectors (KIDs) rather than TES bolometers.

SO:Japan. A low-frequency SAT (27/39 GHz) contributed by Japan, targeting synchrotron radiation foreground characterization — complementing the UHF SAT's dust characterization at high frequencies.

the UHF small aperture telescope

SAT-UHF observes at 220 and 280 GHz — the highest-frequency telescope in the SO array. These bands are not sensitive to the primordial B-mode signal directly; instead, they are critical for characterizing and subtracting Galactic thermal dust emission, which peaks in this frequency range and is the dominant polarized foreground for B-mode science at SO's target angular scales. Without the UHF bands, constraints on r from the MF SATs would be fundamentally limited by foreground systematics rather than detector noise [Hertig et al. 2024].

SAT-UHF fields over 12,000 TES bolometers using lenslet-coupled sinuous antenna detector arrays read out via microwave SQUID multiplexing (μMUX). The 280 GHz band is partially attenuated by atmospheric water vapor, placing stringent demands on optical efficiency and detector noise. The detector array architecture — including microstrip coupling, bandpass filters, and bolometer design — is described in detail in chapter 5 of my thesis.

my contributions: integration, deployment, and commissioning

I built, integrated, tested, deployed, and commissioned SAT-UHF as part of the UC Berkeley group. The telescope was assembled at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory in a staged integration sequence: mechanical validation, thermal and vacuum testing, CHWP commissioning, and full detector array integration and characterization. The strategy — informed by lessons from SAT-MF1 — reduced on-site integration time and systematic risk. Every stage of the integration and testing matrix is documented in [Mangu et al. 2024] and chapter 4 of my thesis.

The final in-lab cooldown of SAT-UHF at LBNL was successful, after which the telescope was packed and shipped to Chile and installed on the platform. Site integration built on the infrastructure and lessons established during SAT-MF1 deployment, whose in-lab beam characterization confirmed it met science requirements before deployment [Gerras et al. 2024].

I am currently working on early science data from SAT-UHF — specifically on detector characterization and optical performance validation during the commissioning phase. This includes understanding the in-situ noise properties, optical efficiency, and beam characteristics of the 220 and 280 GHz detector arrays as the instrument moves toward full science operations.
