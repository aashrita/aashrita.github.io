6 Observing the CMB and Future Work

Experiments like SO, CMB-S4, BICEP/Keck Array, SPT-3G, and CCAT-Prime are attempting to probe the CMB at unprecedented sensitivities from the ground. As discussed in Section \(\ref{sec:ch3:sensitivity}\), observation strategies are a critical component of reaching science goals, and key considerations must be taken to keep foreground contamination low. Recall from Section \(\ref{sec:ch3:sensitivity}\) that the map depth is proportional to noise equivalent temperature (NET), which quantifies the CMB fluctuation in temperature needed to achieve an incident signal power such that the signal-to-noise ratio = 1 in 1 Hz of bandwidth:

\[\sigma_{map} \propto NET_{array}\sqrt{\frac{f_{sky}}{t_{obs}}},\]

The map depth is essentially the map-domain white noise31 converted from the time-domain NET. The rate at which noise is reduced is quantified by the mapping speed, which is inversely proportional to \(NET^2\). Thus, smaller sky fraction \(f_{sky}\) (a fraction of the total sky area) allows for greater integration time (which directly improves NET) at the cost of larger cosmic variance. Of course, these equations are simplified versions of reality, wherein the instrument hardware considerations, scan strategy, calibration choices, and post-processing and analysis affect the final sensitivities achieved. In this chapter, we describe some of these considerations in the Simons Observatory, with a greater emphasis on the small aperture telescopes.

Experiments searching for the faint inflationary B-mode signals (e.g. BICEP/Keck array) observe small sky areas that allow statistically significant observations of large angular scales (low \(\ell\)) in the lowest galactic emission patches available. Telescopes that target smaller angular scales (higher \(\ell\)), on the other hand, often deal with science cases where statistical significance improves with larger sky area. Thus, they often have much larger throws32. The large sky area coverage measures some finite number of sky modes to reduce sample variance. These large sky patch, small scale science scan strategies are less targeted and more optimized for sky coverage than the telescopes performing deep surveys on small sky patches for large angular scales.

Common low galactic foreground regions used by CMB observatories are shown in Figure \(\ref{fig:ch6:scanningfields}\). South Pole experiments enjoy a featureless horizon and constant access to the CMB patch since the sky only rotates in azimuth (because the celestial sphere south pole and zenith are roughly aligned). Additionally, the sun stays low in the horizon at all times (and is completely below the horizon for \(\sim6\) months), favoring high elevation scans. Chilean experiments have an advantage of greater sky coverage, but must combat more complex ground pick up and less uniform atmosphere. Additionally, scan strategies must be optimized to observe the CMB patch since it rises and sets. Sun and moon avoidance must also be optimized in the constant elevation scans done by Chilean experiments. Thus, Chilean observation strategies are a complex process, and SO will dedicate time during initial science observations (ISO) to optimize the scan strategy, particularly for the SATs.

6.1 Overview of SO Baseline Scan Strategy

As shown in Figure \(\ref{fig:ch6:soscansurvey}\), the nominal SO plan is for the SATs to observe \(\sim 10\%\) of the sky and the LAT to observe \(\sim 40\%\) of the sky, avoiding areas with high galactic emission. The LAT overlaps with the Rubin Observatory Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) and the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) surveys, as shown in the figure. The LAT chooses a larger sky coverage rather than smaller, deeper maps overlapping with the SAT to enable reaching the high sensitivities required for high \(\ell\) science and transient searches. The baseline LAT strategy involves constant elevation scans at a speed of \(1^{\circ}/s\).

The original proposed baseline SAT scan strategy (see Figure \(\ref{fig:ch6:baselinesatscan}\)) utilized an opportunistic scheduler that covered southern and northern patches in low foreground areas. This opportunistic scheduler built a schedule by prioritizing targets given some base conditions (e.g. observation elevations, sun and moon avoidance distances, observing sky patch), as described in (Goeckner-Wald et al. 2018). However, although this scheduler yielded better efficiency, it was changing the azimuth and elevation very frequently. In the field, this would affect our ability to integrate down and mitigate scan synchronous signals, a primary systematic in B-mode science.

The calibration observations in the opportunistic scheduler are fitted into gaps in the schedule (e.g. when the CMB patch is not at the desired elevation). This baseline had the caveats of giving no penalty for changing elevations, no restrictions on the number of elevations observed, and no restriction in changes in the azimuth range (Goeckner-Wald et al. 2018). These are important limitations in real science observations. Moving the telescope (such as during changing elevations) can heat up the focal plane from vibration, and requires a settling time before a scan can begin. Small things like scan speed need to be carefully considered (see e.g. on \(\ref{sec:ch5:tesreadout}\) on detector time constants). While the opportunistic scheduler is not being used for SO scan strategy, it provided a useful starting point for developing the current schedule.

The improved current baseline for the SAT builds the schedule by prioritizing CMB and calibration targets and including similar base conditions as the opportunistic scheduler (e.g. sun and moon avoidance). We prioritize integration time in the following order: the southern field first, then the northern field, and finally the galaxy. The schedule uses time efficiently by observing the galaxy at the same azimuth and elevation when the CMB patch is unavailable. (Additionally, some targets will interrupt the CMB field.) Calibration schedules are optimized to minimize loss of CMB observation time. This current strategy uses constant elevation scans, has four set azimuth ranges, and, where possible, changes boresight angle daily between \(\pm45^{\circ}\) and \(0^{\circ}\). All of these considerations were set to minimize scan synchronous signals and effectively deal with systematics. More details are provided in the next section.

6.2 Ongoing SAT Calibration and Scan Strategy Work

Current work includes building a framework to take SAT-UHF time-ordered data (TOD) to maps once the SAT starts scanning the sky. Much of this work has already been developing while the other MF SATs have come online for preliminary sky scans. Additionally, modeling and mitigating ground pick up for the SATs will be a key aspect of systematics testing. Ground pick up (also known as scan synchronous signals discussed earlier) couples to the scan strategy and has direct impacts on the ability to constrain r. Following a commissioning period, the scan strategy baseline review occurs roughly 3 months into initial science observations (ISO). Refer to Figure \(\ref{fig:ch6:isoflowchart}\). A brief summary of ISO goals are:

  • Pre-processing of the data will involve cleaning up raw timestreams. Cosmic ray hits, glitches, and jumps are examples of what gets masked or fixed at the TOD level. Data is also cut due to issues such as loading, poor scans, or poor calibration data. This is the point at which scan synchronous signals are corrected, and areas with higher noise can be masked off along with point sources. Instrument calibration will be used to remove known systematics.

  • Mapmaking uses the pre-processed data with the pointing and noise model to use a filter and bin mapmaker. (The LAT will use a maximum likelihood mapmaker with a noise model built off of ACT’s noise model.)

  • Systematics testing will involve jackknife (i.e. null) tests for each systematic, including rising versus setting scans, left vs right scans, varying elevations and azimuths scans, precipitable water vapor (PWV) scan separation, wafer-to-wafer splits, and polarization splits. At this point, if the tests fail, then we must return to pre-processing. Refining this testing period to fail less is a key goal of ISO.

  • Systematics residuals involve propagating uncertainties in the data due to known systematics to power spectrum uncertainties (once systematics testing is passed).

  • Multi-frequency maps are then made to effectively subtract non-gaussian contributions from the foregrounds. The galaxy emission is often masked out to not greatly bias the noise matrix.

Details of the observation strategy are being finalized as we enter science observation periods this year. This refinement in scan strategy during the currently ongoing commissioning period will require testing for instrumental effects. We plan to compare noise and scan synchronous signals at with different parameters and then select a baseline. The current considerations for commissioning are:

  • Mount Speeds33: 0.8, 1.5 degrees/s

  • Mount Accelerations (at turnarounds): 1, 2, 3 degrees/s\(^2\)

  • Elevations: 50, 60 degrees

  • Boresight angles: -45, 0, 45 degrees

This will also inform detector thermal settling times after an elevation slew, as mentioned earlier. From these commissioning tests, we will then pick the fastest acceleration feasible for the primary chosen scan speeds. Two scan speeds will be carried into ISO, which will be useful for additional systematics testing (e.g. cryogenic half wave plate data analysis or other time constant-dependent testing). Additionally, ISO will use \(60^{\circ}\) elevation, but \(50^{\circ}\) elevation will be tested as a back up. A hybrid sun and moon sidelobe measurement approach will be prioritized. Sidelobes at angles \(>45^{\circ}\) for the sun and angles \(<45^{\circ}\) for the moon will be measured to determine the sun and moon avoidance for each SAT. Different baffling and detectors between SATs required independent determination of these angles for each. ISO will also help determine if more or less than the three boresight angles are required.

ISO data products will include:

  • Maps and power spectra

  • Sun-centered maps, moon-centered maps, ground maps and phase maps

  • Scan, transfer functions, null and consistency spectra meta-data

After the roughly 3 months of using the baseline scan strategy, key scan strategy parameters will be assessed including the scan speed, boresight angles and switching cadence, sun and moon avoidance radii, scan throw, calibration cadence, and elevation. The baseline SAT scan strategy is summarized as follows, with the understanding that the strategy can be optimized further with regular maintenance and calibration (and that ISO will help determine the rest of the scan parameters discussed earlier):

  • Elevation: 60 degrees

  • Azimuth throw (on mount): 40 degrees

  • Scan style: SW/SE/NW/NE, with four repeating central azimuth pointing (\(75^{\circ}\), \(120^{\circ}\), \(240^{\circ}\), \(285^{\circ}\)). When the southern field is unavailable, the north will be scanned. If neither is available, the galaxy will be observed at the SE/SW. If that is unavailable, then the galaxy will be observed at the NW/NE. Prioritization of southern field directly improves \(r\) constraints. See Figure \(\ref{fig:ch6:newbaselinesatscan}\). Azimuth scans with boresight centers around (minimum azimuth, maximum azimuth) are:

    • SW: (208.51,248.51)

    • SE: (111.49, 151.49)

    • NW: (270.89, 310.89)

    • NE: (49.11, 89.11)

  • Rising and setting scans will be equally observed and prioritized. This prioritization will depend on the sun and moon avoidance determined during ISO.

  • The cryogenic half wave plate rotation direction will change every week during regular operation

  • If the SAT is able to boresight rotate, then it will be done daily.

ISO will be an exciting time for SO, and we will learn much about the instrument and optimal Chilean observations strategies from these efforts in the coming months. This work will also be applicable to SO:UK, SO:Japan, ASO, and CMB-S4 in the coming years.

Modern CMB experiments such as the Simons Observatory (SO) and CMB-S4 aim to investigate models of early universe inflation, constrain the sum of neutrino masses, probe the nature of dark energy and dark matter, and search for new relic particles beyond the Standard Model using instrumentation with unprecedented sensitivities. In this dissertation, we reviewed:

  • the motivation behind this work and provided an overview of the dissertation

  • a cosmological overview to contextualize the science goals and drivers

  • an overview of modern CMB experiments and sensitivities, with a focus on SO

  • a report of how a small aperture telescope for SO was integrated, tested, and deployed at the Chilean site, along with an overview of current and future work at the site

  • a review of focal plane structures and developing detector arrays in CMB experiments, with an emphasis on technologies developed at Berkeley for SO along with CMB-S4 and LiteBIRD

  • a summary on current and ongoing calibration and scan strategy work to get SO into a successful observation period

This work has successfully pushed one of the four telescopes (and the only ultra-high frequency small aperture telescope) for SO to deployment, developed and optimized detector arrays for current and future CMB experiments, and started building important frameworks for initial science observations with SO telescopes. This is an exciting time to be a part of the CMB community, and SO will be starting full science observations soon with much exciting development happening with upcoming experiments like CMB-S4!
